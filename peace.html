Why Peace: Meaning

Concept, definitions, inner & outer peace

📌 Topic Overview

Peace is far more than the absence of war. It spans cultural, philosophical, spiritual, and political dimensions. Understanding what peace means is the first step to building a peaceful world.

🧘 Inner Peace

  • Peace within oneself — derived from training the mind
  • Addresses worry, anxiety, greed, desire, hatred, ill-will, delusion
  • Achieved through prayer, meditation, wisdom
  • Regarded as true peace and the real foundation of social peace

  • Refers to peace in the external environment
  • Cluttered, chaotic environments hinder inner peace
  • Calm, organized surroundings support wellbeing
  • "Inner peace begins with outer peace"
  • We must take responsibility for outer peace too

Peace Education

Betty Reardon's framework and social transformation

📚 What is Peace Education?

"Peace education seeks to transform the present human condition by changing social structures and patterns of thought that have created it."

— Betty Reardon, Comprehensive Peace Education, 1988

Main purposes: elimination of social injustice, rejection of violence, and abolition of war.

— Reardon & Cabezudo, Learning to Abolish War, 2002

🔄 Contribution to Social Transformation

Fostering Understanding +
Peace education promotes deep understanding of root causes of conflicts and violence. It helps individuals comprehend complexities of social issues, promoting empathy and tolerance among diverse groups.
Conflict Resolution Skills +
Equips individuals with nonviolent conflict resolution skills — crucial for fostering positive social changes and enabling people to address disagreements without resorting to violence.
Promoting Social Justice +
Educates about structural arrangements that produce and legitimize injustice. Encourages critical analysis — empowers individuals to challenge systems that perpetuate social disparities.
Empowering Young People +
Provides new perspectives on conflict resolution, essential skills (critical thinking, communication, empathy), and values of cooperation, respect, and social responsibility.

🛠️ Nonviolent Alternatives for Managing Conflict

💬

Negative Peace

Absence of direct violence; approaches to peace

📌 Definition

Negative Peace refers to a situation where there is no ongoing violence or open conflict, resulting in a temporary calm (often a cease-fire). However, this state does not address the underlying issues that initially led to the conflict.
⚠️ Negative peace ≠ True Peace. It only deals with the absence of direct physical violence. Root causes, inequalities, and structural issues persist.

✅ Why Negative Peace is Better than War

  • Avoidance of Direct Harm: No physical harm; protects lives, minimizes casualties
  • Potential for Stability: Creates temporary calm; opportunity for recovery
  • Diplomatic Window: Space for negotiations and dialogue

❌ Limitations of Negative Peace

  • Does not address root causes of conflict
  • Risk of renewed tensions always present
  • Falls short on social justice & equality
  • Cultural & structural violence may persist
  • Galtung: Cannot achieve genuine, lasting peace

📋 External Examples of Negative Peace

🔺 Three Approaches to Peace (Galtung's Framework)

🛡️ Peacekeeping

Focus: Behaviour / Physical Violence

Deployment of neutral third-party military/civilian force to monitor conflicts, supervise ceasefires, implement peace agreements.

Goal: Create stable, secure environment

🕊️ Peacemaking

Focus: Attitudes / Cultural Violence

Diplomatic efforts & negotiations to bring conflicting parties to negotiation table and address underlying issues.

Goal: Find common ground; broker agreements

🏗️ Peacebuilding

Focus: Structural Violence / Contradictions

Comprehensive long-term process addressing structural & societal issues: governance, economic development, social cohesion, institutions.

Goal: Lasting peace by addressing root causes

🧘 Inner Negative Peace

Cultivating inner negative peace (addressing internal conflicts; attaining inner calm) is crucial for:

💬
Conflict Resolution

Approach disagreements with clear, composed mindset; effective communication & active listening

💙
Emotional Well-being

Meditation & mindfulness; reduced stress, improved self-regulation, overall sense of well-being

👨‍👩‍👦

Positive Peace

Presence of justice, wellness, and development

✨ Definition

Positive Peace signifies a state marked by the nonexistence of both actual and potential violence, along with the absence of indirect and structural violence. It surpasses simply lacking conflict, embracing numerous positive elements.

✅ What Positive Peace Means

🚫
Absence of Actual & Potential Violence

Neither present violence nor the threat of future violence

🔗
Absence of Indirect Violence

Elimination of discrimination, social inequalities, non-physical forms of harm

🏛️
Absence of Structural Violence

Dismantling systemic injustices in social, economic, political structures

⚖️
Presence of Justice

Fair legal systems, social justice, safeguarding human rights for all

💚
Opportunities for Wellness

Mental & physical health, access to healthcare, sense of security

📈
Conditions for Development

Addressing economic disparities, fostering education, social & economic progress

Violence addressedDirect violence (war, torture, abuse)Structural & socio-cultural violence

🎯 How Positive Peace Ends Three Types of Violence

Ending Direct Violence +
  • Preventing Conflicts: Targets root causes; emphasizes diplomacy and mediation
  • Social Justice: Tackles inequalities that trigger direct violence
  • Building Communities: Connected communities prevent interpersonal violence
Ending Structural Violence +
  • Economic Development: Reduces structural inequalities; addresses poverty
  • Reforming Institutions: Legal, education, and governance reform; ending discrimination
  • Empowering through Education: Breaks down barriers; fosters tolerance
Ending Cultural Violence +
  • Celebrating Diversity: Fosters understanding and respect across cultures
  • Cultural Exchange: Breaks down stereotypes and prejudices
  • Promoting Tolerance: Education and awareness to counter cultural violence
06

Types of Violence

Galtung's framework: Direct, Structural, Cultural

👨‍🔬 Johan Galtung — Father of Peace Science

Violence is the opposite of peace — like disease is opposite to health. War is NOT the antithesis of peace; rather, violence is its opposite.

— Johan Galtung, 1969
Galtung's Core Idea: Violence prevents human beings from achieving their physical and mental potential. It manifests in three forms: Direct, Structural, and Cultural — each reinforcing the other.

🔺 Galtung's Triangle of Violence

Direct ViolenceVISIBLE

War, murder, assault, torture, child & woman abuse

Structural ViolenceINVISIBLE

Poverty, hunger, inequality, discrimination built into systems

Cultural ViolenceINVISIBLE

Racism, sexism, religious intolerance legitimizing violence

⚡ Direct vs Indirect Violence

AspectDirect ViolenceIndirect Violence
DefinitionIntentional use of physical/psychological force to harmHarm from systemic, societal, or structural factors
CausationIntentional actionsSystemic/structural factors, not necessarily intentional
ImmediacyImmediate & visible consequencesIndirect & often delayed consequences
ObservabilityObservable & apparentNot immediately noticeable
ScaleIndividual or collective levelSystemic; affects larger societal structures
ExamplesPhysical assault, homicide, verbal abuse, war, bullyingDiscriminatory policies, economic disparities, unequal access to education/healthcare

🎯 Intentionality in Direct Violence

Unintended Consequences

Harm occurs but was NOT the intended outcome.

Example: A parent shaking a baby to stop crying — not intending brain damage but causing it.

Intention Without Recognition of Violence

Consequences were intended, but perpetrators don't recognize their behavior as violent.

Example: Physical punishment in certain educational traditions may not be considered violence by perpetrators.

⭐ Key Thinker

Johan Galtung is the "Father of Peace Science." His 1969 essay "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research" introduced the concepts of structural violence, negative peace, and positive peace. Always cite him!

07

Structural Violence

Systemic injustice embedded in social structures

🏛️ Definition

Structural Violence (coined by Johan Galtung, 1969) refers to societal harm or injustice embedded in structures and institutions of a society — operating subtly through systemic mechanisms, resulting in unequal access to resources, opportunities, and basic needs.

"Structural violence is injustice and exploitation built into a social system that generates wealth for the few and poverty for the many, stunting everyone's ability to develop their full humanity."

— Hathaway, 2013

🔍 5 Key Characteristics of Structural Violence

1
Indirect Nature

Harm does not result from immediate, intentional actions — rooted in broader systemic conditions

2
Depersonalized Perpetrator

No clearly identifiable perpetrator — it is woven into the fabric of systemic structures

3
Inherent in Power Structures

Permeates social, economic, and political systems — resistant to easy eradication

4
Unequal Access to Opportunities

Disparity in education, healthcare, employment — creating systemic disadvantages for specific groups

5
Invisibility & Normalization

Operates covertly; becomes normalized and routinized within societal structures

🗂️ Six Forms of Structural Violence

🏷️ Caste

Systemic discrimination based on social caste; constrains opportunities for specific caste groups

💼 Class

Inequities in opportunities linked to socioeconomic class; unequal wealth, education, employment

♀️ Gender

Systemic inequalities faced based on gender; unequal access to education, employment, healthcare

🎭 Racism

Systemic discrimination rooted in race/ethnicity; ingrained in social institutions

🍽️ Hunger

Uneven distribution of food resources; socioeconomic conditions & policies contributing to food insecurity

📉 Poverty

Systematic disadvantages due to economic inequalities; limited access to education, healthcare, jobs

🏗️ Understanding Social Structure

Status

Socially defined position within a group based on age, sex, qualifications, economic power, education

Rights & Duties

Entitlements and responsibilities associated with a particular status

Role

Expected behavior associated with someone occupying a particular status

⭐ Exam Important

Always state that structural violence was introduced by Johan Galtung in 1969 in "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research." Mention the 5 characteristics and 6 forms — these are frequently tested.

08

Cultural Violence

Legitimization of structural & direct violence through culture

🎭 Definition & Impact

Cultural violence refers to aspects of culture (religion, ideology, language, art, empirical science) that can be used to legitimize direct or structural violence in its direct or structural form. It makes violence appear "normal" or "justified."

Cultural violence operates covertly — integrating deeply into societal norms, making it difficult to identify and question. It perpetuates biased ideologies: racism, sexism, religious intolerance.

🔧 Four Tools of Cultural Violence

1
Cultural Norms

Prevalent societal beliefs that shape acceptable behavior. When norms endorse discrimination, they normalize prejudiced attitudes — contributing to a culture of inequality.

2
Stereotypes & Prejudices

Fixed, oversimplified ideas about certain groups. Dehumanize individuals based on characteristics, perpetuating cultural violence.

3
Racist or Sexist Language

Language reinforces or challenges cultural norms. Racist/sexist language reflects and reinforces biased beliefs.

4
Language-Based Discrimination

Targeting individuals based on language; reinforces social hierarchies and power imbalances; leads to exclusionary practices.

🔗 Relationship: Cultural ↔ Structural Violence

1. Reinforcement Mechanism +
Cultural violence can strengthen and legitimize structural violence. Biased cultural beliefs are used to justify unfair policies. Example: Cultural narratives perpetuating stereotypes are used to justify discriminatory institutional practices.
2. Normalization of Inequality +
Cultural violence makes discriminatory practices seem normal, supporting structural violence by embedding biased attitudes into societal norms. Example: Cultural acceptance of gender discrimination normalizes unequal treatment of women.
3. Resistance & Change +
Changes in cultural norms can challenge structural violence; conversely, changes in structural policies influence cultural perceptions. Example: Increased awareness of racial equality leads to changes in laws challenging structural racism.
4. Intersectionality +
Cultural and structural violence intersect with direct violence — creating intricate webs of oppression. Example: Discrimination based on race AND socioeconomic status creates disparities in education, health, and jobs.

Vedas & Upanishads

Ancient wisdom — structure, philosophy, Mahavakyas

📜 Introduction to the Vedas

  • Root word: Sanskrit 'vid' = knowledge
  • Apaurusheya: Not authored by human beings — divine origin
  • Shruti: "Heard" — belonging to oral tradition
  • Compiled by: Sage Krishna Dwaipayana Vyasa (Veda Vyasa)
  • Four Vedas: Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva
  • Assistants in compilation: Paila (Rig), Vaisampayana (Yajur), Jaimini (Sama), Sumantu (Atharva)

🗂️ Structure of Each Veda (Two Parts)

Karma Kanda (Action)

Samhitas: Collections of Mantras (hymns to gods for material favor)

Brahmanas: Elaborate ritualism; rules for performing yajnyas (sacrifices)

Jnyan Kanda (Knowledge)

Aranyakas: Composed in forests; transition from ritualism to free inquiry; mystical interpretation of sacrifices

Upanishads: Highly philosophical; cream of Vedic philosophy = "Vedanta"

🧘 The Upanishads

Upanishad = "Upa-ni-sad" = "to sit nearby" — teachings imparted by a guru to students sitting at his side. Also means "that which takes one to Brahman."

Major Upanishads (Adi Shankaracharya's 9): Isha, Kena, Katha, Prashna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittirīya, Chandogya, Brihadaranyaka

Central teaching: The ultimate Reality — Brahman/Atman

📖 Key Upanishads & Their Teachings

Isha Upanishad (Shukla Yajurveda) +
Brahman is One — does not move yet faster than mind; far yet near; outward yet inward. Doctrine of the infinite. "Everything pervaded by the Lord."
Kena Upanishad (Samaveda) +
What impels senses & mind? Brahman sustains all but nothing sustains it. "He who says he knows it does not really know it." Story of Yaksha — quelling of pride before self-realization.
Katha Upanishad (Samaveda) +
Knowledge of Atman — smaller than smallest, greater than greatest. Story of Nachiketa & Yama. Two paths: Shreyas (good) vs Preyas (pleasant). Body = chariot, Soul = rider, Intellect = charioteer, Mind = reins, Senses = horses.
Mandukya Upanishad (Atharvaveda) +
Only 12 mantras — "Mandukya alone is enough for liberation." Explains OM (AUM). Atman has 4 states: Jagrat (waking), Svapna (dream), Sushupti (deep sleep), Turiya (pure state).
Chandogya Upanishad (Rigveda) +
Everything is Brahman. Contains "Tat tvam asi" (That art thou) — Uddalaka teaches son Shvetaketu. Rivers merging into ocean = everything entering Atman.
Taittiriya Upanishad (Samaveda) +
Five Koshas (sheaths of self): Annamaya (food), Pranamaya (breath), Manomaya (mind), Vijnanamaya (knowledge), Anandamaya (bliss). Brahman = Truth, Consciousness, Infinite.

Buddhism

Tripitaka, Dhammapada, peace philosophy

☸️ Buddhist Literature Structure

Canonical Texts

Buddha's teachings (word of the Buddha). Main divisions = Pitakas. Together = Tripitakas ("Three Baskets" in Pali).

Also called Sutras (Sanskrit) or Suttas (Pali)

Non-Canonical Texts

Observations and commentaries on canonical texts in Pali, Tibetan, Chinese, and other languages. Composed largely by Sri Lankan monks.

Includes historical chronicles like Dipavamsa, Mahavamsa, Culavamsa

📚 The Tripitaka (Three Baskets)

1
Vinaya Pitaka (Discipline Basket)

Rules & guidelines of monastic life. 227 regulations for monastic discipline. From basic moral principles to interaction guidelines between monks, nuns & ordinary people.

2
Sutta Pitaka (Discourse Basket)

Largest basket. Discourses of Buddha & close disciples. Also called Buddhavacana (word of the Buddha). Contains Dhammapada — most widely known Buddhist text.

3
Abhidharma Pitaka (Special Doctrines Basket)

Poetry, songs & stories about Buddha's life. 7 works on philosophy and doctrine. Some Buddhist schools don't consider it canonical.

📖 Dhammapada — "The Buddha's Path to Wisdom"

Most widely known Buddhist text. 26 chapters, 423 verses. Focuses on true nature of existence and the path to liberation. Teachings based on Buddha's understanding of reality. Translated by Max Muller in 1870.

Core Goal: Free people from the evils of hatred, passion and ignorance — to achieve "conquest of self."

💬 Important Buddhist Quotes & Explanations

"All that we are, is the result of what we have thought... If one speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows one, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox."

— Dhammapada, Verse 1

Every experience begins with a thought. Evil acts/thoughts follow us, causing unpleasant experiences. Good thoughts and deeds bring happiness like a shadow.

"Better than a thousand hollow words is one useful word, hearing which one attains peace."

— Dhammapada

Minimalism: one meaningful thought is better than a thousand empty ones. Conquest of self is better than conquest of others.

🔑 Five Philosophical Beliefs of Mahavira

A. Belief in Soul & Karma +
Every living creature has a soul. Soul is in bondage due to karma — can be liberated by disintegrating karmic force and freeing from worldly attachments. Soul (atma) becomes Parmatama when it attains infinite greatness.
B. Nirvana (Salvation) +
Ultimate objective of life is salvation. Avoid evil karmas; prevent new evil karmas. Follow five vows + right conduct, right knowledge, right faith.
C. Non-belief in God +
Universe has no start or end; no creator or destroyer. Universe simply changes form. Man is the maker and destroyer of his own destiny — liberation does not depend on any outside authority.
D. Ahimsa (Non-violence) +
Even animals, plants, stones and rocks have life and shouldn't be harmed. Jains put end to sacrifices by popularizing ahimsa.
E. Freedom of Women +
Right to attain nirvana is equally important for women. Mahavira believed in equality; was not a believer in the caste system.

💎 Three Jewels (Tri-Ratna) of Jainism

Right Belief

Rational attitude; renouncing superstitious beliefs; understand true nature of substances in universe. Faith in Tirthankaras' teachings.

Right Knowledge

True & relevant knowledge of reality. Knowledge of 6 universal substances (soul, matter, motion, rest, space, time) and 9 tattvas.

Right Conduct

Proper, appropriate, honest conduct. Attain state of perfect equanimity. Liberate from attachment, aversion, impure thoughts/words/deeds.

📚 Jain Literature

Agam Literature (Canonical)

Written in Prakrit. Original scriptures compiled by Gandharas and elder monks (Srut-kevalis). Divided into: Ang Agams (direct teachings of Mahavira) & Angbahya Agams (commentaries).

Non-Agam Literature (Non-Canonical)

Written in Prakrit, Sanskrit, old Marathi, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, German, English. Commentaries and independent works of scholars.

📌 Digambar sect believes all 26 Agam sutras were lost. Shwetambar sect accepts the documented scriptures. These two sects have opposing views on canonical texts.
⭐ Frequently Compared

Similarities between Buddhism & Jainism: Both emphasize non-violence (ahimsa), both have Three Jewels/Eightfold Path, both emphasize liberation (Nirvana/Moksha), both reject the caste system, both founded by royal princes who renounced wealth.

💰 Artha — Four Forms of Wealth

📚
Knowledge

Greatest wealth — both material (worldly life) and spiritual (self-realization)

💪
Health

Good food, proper exercise, good thoughts = physical, emotional, mental well-being

😌
Contentment

Abstinence from excessive desire; satisfaction with honest effort results

💎
Material Wealth

Acquired within Dharma; used for charitable purposes; not for greed/luxury

❤️ Kama — Understanding Desires

Controlled & Beneficial Desire ✅

Acts as catalytic agent for actions. Personal and worldly progress results from desire to achieve and discover.

Uncontrolled/Harmful Desire ❌

Can lead to destruction. Like fire to which fuel is added — grows more and more with indulgence.

⚠️ Desires arising from lust, greed, and anger should be curbed absolutely. Control through wisdom & discrimination — NOT suppression (suppressed desires bounce back).

🕊️ Moksha — Final Liberation

  • State of Ananda (perfect bliss) — nothing more remains to be attained
  • Extremely difficult — typically takes efforts on many births
  • Requires: arduous spiritual practice, constant & unselfish devotion, non-attachment
  • No instant Moksha — must first go through Dharma, Artha & Kama
  • Only humans, with higher intelligence, can reach beyond animal level to spiritual goals
  • Sanyasa (renunciation from the beginning) is NOT recommended except in rare cases

⚠️ Common Exam Mistakes

❌ "Negative peace is bad peace"
✅ Negative peace is the ABSENCE of direct violence. It is better than war, but not sufficient for true peace.
❌ Confusing Peacekeeping and Peacebuilding
✅ Peacekeeping = immediate (stop fighting) | Peacemaking = diplomatic (negotiate) | Peacebuilding = long-term (address root causes)
❌ Saying Galtung said "war is the opposite of peace"
✅ Galtung said VIOLENCE (not war) is the opposite of peace — like disease is opposite to health.
❌ Thinking Jainism believes in a creator God
✅ Mahavira believed the universe has no creator or destroyer. Man is the maker of his own destiny.
❌ Confusing Atman and Brahman
✅ Atman = individual soul (wave) | Brahman = universal absolute reality (ocean) | Ayam atma brahma = they are the same
❌ Forgetting who compiled the Vedas
✅ Sage Krishna Dwaipayana Vyasa (Veda Vyasa) compiled the single mass of Veda into four Vedas.
